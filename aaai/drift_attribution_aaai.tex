\begin{abstract}
\todo{Write last. One paragraph: (1) monitoring detects a change in a deployed
clinical model's performance but does not explain it; (2) attribution methods
decompose a performance change into distribution-shift mechanisms with a
cooperative game whose coalition values switch mechanisms between the two
periods; (3) once the model is deployed and acted upon, the alert policy has no
counterpart before deployment: each coalition value is a domain-adaptation
problem with the pre-deployment period as source, absolute continuity fails or
nearly fails where only the alert triggers treatment, so the game is not
identified from pre-deployment data or not usefully estimable, and its
estimator can fail silently with a bias that does not shrink with sample size;
(4) we order the model-mediated mechanism last
(an asymmetric Shapley value), so that its share is a policy contrast,
identified by a randomised unalerted arm (a holdout set in the sense of Liley et al.); when deployment also
moves the patient mix, the arm cannot separate that shift from exogenous change,
which a site-level stepped wedge with random rollout order can; (5) in
simulation the standard decomposition books the alert's
success to outcome-mechanism shift and reverses the keep-or-refit decision,
while the policy-contrast decomposition recovers its own estimand; on
semi-synthetic MIMIC-IV ward admissions (real covariates, outcomes and
temporal drift, simulated alert) the monitor books the alert's effect to the
outcome mechanism as an apparent improvement that hides most of a real
deterioration, and a randomised arm recovers it.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\todo{Four paragraphs, about one page.}

\paragraph{P1: the monitoring gap.} After deployment, a clinical prediction
model's measured performance changes. Monitoring reports that it changed, not
why. The change can come from three mechanisms that demand different
responses: the patient population changed (covariate shift), the outcome
mechanism changed (biology, care pathways, coding and documentation), or the
model worked: high-risk patients were flagged, clinicians intervened, outcomes
improved, and the learned predictor-outcome relationship was invalidated by its
own success~\citep{lenert2019victims, sperrin2019explicit,
balcarcel2025feedback}. Monitoring data show the first, because covariates are
observed in both periods, but cannot separate the second from the third. The
last mechanism lowers observed
discrimination at every event rate we simulate and on MIMIC-IV ward admissions
(F7), while the Brier score rises or falls with the event rate
(Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{P2: attribution exists and is solved for the exogenous case.}
\citet{zhang2023attributing} formulate performance-change
attribution as a cooperative game whose players are causal mechanisms and
quantify each mechanism's contribution as its Shapley value; \citet{budhathoki2021rootcauses} attribute distribution change to individual
mechanisms; \citet{cai2023disde} decompose a drop into $Y|X$ and $X$
terms; \citet{gordaliza2025causal} carry the framework into
medical imaging. \emph{We do not claim that nobody decomposes drift into
causal components.}

\paragraph{P3: deployment feedback breaks identification.} The game values a
set of mechanisms by the performance that would have been observed had only
that set changed between the two periods. Under deployment feedback one
mechanism, the action policy, is induced by the audited model and has no
counterpart before deployment: patients whom only the alert would flag were
almost never treated under standard care. \citet{boeken2024evaluating} model
the deployment of a decision support system as a causal domain shift; they
define deployment and retraining effects, show that these are not identifiable
from a single source domain without further assumptions, and identify them
when the action is measured after deployment and an absolute-continuity
condition holds. \citet{liley2021model} show that naive updating of a score
that drives interventions either converges to a score that predicts its own
effect or settles into an oscillation, propose a holdout set of patients for
whom the score is not computed as a safe update strategy, and name as an open
difficulty telling whether a model's inaccuracy comes from drift in the
underlying system or from the effect of the intervention. That difficulty is
the question of this paper. \citet{haidarwehbe2025holdout} derive the optimal
size of such a holdout set, and \citet{feng2024designing} design monitoring
strategies that account for performativity. Treatment that follows a prediction
makes the model look worse at validation~\citep{pajouheshnia2017accounting}
and can make a deployed model look decayed~\citep{ansari2025challenges};
feedback-aware monitors estimate the model's performance despite the feedback
by randomising or weighting~\citep{corbin2023avoiding, kim2025monitoring}.
Prediction-under-interventions work
defines estimands for a model whose predictions trigger
treatment~\citep{sperrin2018msm, vangeloven2020prediction,
keogh2024prediction, luijken2024estimands}, and the performative-prediction
literature studies retraining under such feedback~\citep{perdomo2020performative,
kabra2024limitations, lee2026partially}. None of this work decomposes an
observed performance change into the contributions of its mechanisms.

\paragraph{P4: contributions.} We make three contributions.
(i)~\emph{Non-identification of the attribution game}
(Section~\ref{sec:break}). The attribution game inherits the
absolute-continuity condition under which \citet{boeken2024evaluating}
identify deployment effects, and loses identification where it fails: each
coalition value of the game of \citet{zhang2023attributing} is a
domain-adaptation problem in their sense, with the pre-deployment period as
source. If
standard care never treats the patients that only the alert flags, absolute
continuity fails there, and the game is not identified from the two periods
without assuming the outcome-mechanism stability it is meant to test; its
importance-weighting estimator then fails silently, with a bias that does not
shrink with sample size, as weighting estimators do under structural
positivity violations~\citep{petersen2012positivity}. If standard care treats
them rarely, the estimator is
consistent but too variable to use.
(ii)~\emph{A decomposition with the model as a policy, identified by design}
(Sections~\ref{sec:estimand} and~\ref{sec:ident}). For this problem the
symmetric split is the wrong one: it gives half of the interaction between the
policy and the covariate shift to the covariate shift, while the keep-or-refit
decision needs the policy's effect in the shifted population. We order the
policy last, an asymmetric Shapley value~\citep{frye2020asymmetric} whose
ordering encodes the decision rather than a causal order; the policy's share is
then a policy contrast, the loss-scale analogue of the deployment effect of
\citet{boeken2024evaluating}. A randomised unalerted arm, the holdout
set of \citet{liley2021model} and \citet{haidarwehbe2025holdout}, identifies
the policy term but cannot separate a covariate shift caused by deployment from
exogenous shift; a site-level stepped wedge with random rollout order can. A
threshold discontinuity, already used to evaluate a deployed deterioration
alert~\citep{gallo2024effectiveness}, identifies only the local effect of the
alert, which equals the policy's average effect only under assumptions on the
effect curve above the threshold that our simulation violates. The designs are
standard; what we add is which term of the decomposition each identifies.
(iii)~\emph{Consequences for decisions, in simulation and on MIMIC-IV}
(Section~\ref{sec:sim}). The standard decomposition books the alert's success
to outcome-mechanism shift, with narrow, well-calibrated intervals for the
wrong estimand, so nothing in its output warns the analyst; a keep-or-refit
rule fed by it refits an effective model and loses the benefit. We reproduce the oscillation of naive retraining predicted by
Theorem~1 of \citet{liley2021model} in an alert-threshold setting and add that
holding the alert rate fixed stops the oscillation, which removes the loss
under a homogeneous treatment effect but still loses a third of the benefit
under a heterogeneous one, and that the dashboard metric and patient
benefit move in antiphase. That observed AUROC falls while the alert averts
more events is known~\citep{pajouheshnia2017accounting, ansari2025challenges},
the mirror image of harmful deployments that leave discrimination
intact~\citep{vanamsterdam2025selffulfilling}; we add that standard net benefit
turns negative with it, while net benefit against standard care, which the
randomised arm identifies, stays positive, a result complementary to
Section~3.1 of \citet{liley2021model}. On semi-synthetic MIMIC-IV ward
admissions, with real covariates, outcomes and temporal drift and a simulated
alert, the monitor again books the alert's effect to the outcome mechanism,
here as an apparent improvement that hides most of a real deterioration; the
randomised arm recovers the policy term, and naive refitting loses far less
than at our synthetic operating point.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Environments.} Two environments: $e_0$ (pre-deployment, model $f$
not acted upon) and $e_1$ (post-deployment). Covariates $X$, risk score
$\rhat = f(X)$, action $A \in \mathcal{A}$ (treatment or escalation), outcome
$Y$. Exogenous temporal factors $U$ may affect $X$, $A$ and $Y$.

\paragraph{Structural model.} In $e_0$, $A \sim \pi_0(a \mid x)$ (standard
care). In $e_1$, $A \sim \pi_1(a \mid x, \rhat)$, where the dependence on
$\rhat$ is the deployment (alert threshold $\tau$ plus clinician response).
Outcomes follow $P_e(Y \mid X, A)$, which may itself differ between
environments through $U$. The graph is
$X \to \rhat \to A \to Y$, $X \to A$, $X \to Y$, with $U$ entering $X$, $A$,
$Y$.

\paragraph{Clinical task.} We fix one task throughout. The population is adult
ward inpatients, scored at a prediction time during the admission; the outcome
$Y$ is deterioration within 12 hours, defined as ICU admission or in-hospital
death. An alert ($\rhat > \tau$) triggers a bedside review by the ward team, who
may start early treatment or escalate care; $A = 1$ denotes that action.
Without the model, standard care acts on clinical suspicion: patients whose
deterioration is apparent at the bedside are reviewed and treated early, and
other patients rarely are. The model adds value for the patients it flags whom
clinicians would not have suspected; before deployment such patients were
almost never treated, which is the support condition that
Section~\ref{sec:break} turns on. The simulator of Section~\ref{sec:sim} encodes
these policies: $\pi_0$ treats suspected patients with probability $0.8$ and
others with probability $\varepsilon$, $\pi_1$ also treats alerted patients whom
clinicians did not suspect with probability $0.85$, and treatment lowers the
log-odds of deterioration by $\theta$. The semi-synthetic MIMIC-IV experiment
(Appendix~\ref{app:mimic}) uses the same population, outcome and horizon.

\paragraph{What monitoring sees.} Routine monitoring evaluates a loss $\ell$ on
$(X, Y)$ pairs and reports
$\Delta R = \E_{e_1}[\ell(f(X), Y)] - \E_{e_0}[\ell(f(X), Y)]$,
marginalising over $A$. Attribution methods decompose $\Delta R$.

\paragraph{The attribution game~\citep{zhang2023attributing}.} Let
$\mathcal{M} = \{m_1, \ldots, m_K\}$ be the mechanisms (conditional
distributions) of a factorisation of $P(X, A, Y)$. For $S \subseteq
\mathcal{M}$, the hybrid distribution $P_S$ takes mechanisms in $S$ from $e_1$
and the rest from $e_0$. The value is $v(S) = R_{P_S}(f) - R_{e_0}(f)$ and the
attribution to $m_k$ is its Shapley value $\phi_k(v)$. The importance-weighting
estimator of $R_{P_S}(f)$ reweights $e_0$ (or $e_1$) samples by the density
ratio of the changed mechanisms.

\section{Why the game breaks under deployment feedback}
\label{sec:break}

\todo{Core of the paper. Two propositions, each with a short proof in the

\begin{assumption}[Independent switchability]
\label{ass:switch}
For every $S \subseteq \mathcal{M}$, $P_S$ is a well-defined distribution and
$R_{P_S}(f)$ is identified from samples of $e_0$ and $e_1$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}[Degenerate pre-deployment policy and positivity failure]
\label{prop:absence}
Let $\mathcal{M} = \{P(X), m_A, m_Y\}$ with action mechanism
$m_A = P(A \mid X, \rhat)$, $m_A^{(0)} = \pi_0(a \mid x)$,
$m_A^{(1)} = \pi_1(a \mid x, \rhat)$, and outcome mechanism
$m_Y = P(Y \mid X, A)$. Suppose that for some action $a$ the set
$\mathcal{X}_a = \{x : \pi_1(a \mid x, f(x)) > 0 = \pi_0(a \mid x)\}$ has positive
probability in both periods (the deployment induces actions standard care never
took). Then for every coalition $S$ with $m_A \in S$ and $m_Y \notin S$,
$R_{P_S}(f)$ depends on $P_{e_0}(Y \mid X = x, A = a)$ for $x \in \mathcal{X}_a$,
which the pre-deployment period never observes: Assumption~\ref{ass:switch}
fails and the importance weights $\pi_1 / \pi_0$ are unbounded on
$\mathcal{X}_a$. Unless the outcome mechanism is assumed unchanged on
$\mathcal{X}_a$, which is part of what the attribution is meant to assess,
$(e_0, e_1)$ data in general identify neither $\phi_{m_A}(v)$ nor
$\phi_{m_Y}(v)$, and identify $\phi_{P(X)}(v)$ only if the covariate
distribution is unchanged on $\mathcal{X}_a$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Reading.} This is the absolute-continuity condition of
\citet{boeken2024evaluating}, applied to each coalition value with the
pre-deployment period as source. The score $\rhat = f(X)$ is computable in
$e_0$, so the action mechanism is not absent before deployment; it is
degenerate in $\rhat$. Taking the union graph of both environments therefore
does not rescue the game: the failure is positivity, not graph coverage. The
counterfactual ``only the policy changed'' asks how the pre-deployment outcome
mechanism would have responded to actions standard care never took; no
pre-deployment sample answers it, and post-deployment samples answer it only
for the post-deployment mechanism. \todo{Remark: performativity enters a second time in the update
step, since $R_{P_S}(f)$ depends on $f$ through $P_S$ as well as through the
loss; any update derived from the attribution changes the environment the
attribution was computed in (Section~\ref{sec:estimand}).}

\begin{proposition}[Marginal attribution misattributes performative success]
\label{prop:misattr}
Suppose the game is run on the marginal factorisation
$\mathcal{M}' = \{P(X), P(Y \mid X)\}$, as in monitoring pipelines that
evaluate $(X, Y)$ pairs without linking alerts to actions. Let the only
difference between $e_0$ and $e_1$ be
$\pi_0 \to \pi_1$, with $P(X)$ and $P(Y \mid X, A)$ unchanged, and let the
model-triggered action reduce risk, $P(Y{=}1 \mid x, a{=}1) < P(Y{=}1 \mid x,
a{=}0)$ on the alerted region $\{x : f(x) \geq \tau\}$. Then
$\phi_{P(X)}(v) = 0$ and $\phi_{P(Y|X)}(v) = \Delta R$: the entire performance
change is attributed to a shift in the outcome mechanism.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Consequence.} The proposition formalises the observation that an
effective intervention can make a deployed model look
decayed~\citep{ansari2025challenges}, and names the mechanism to which a
monitor books the decay. A developer acting on this attribution retrains
$f$ on $e_1$ data. The retrained model learns $\E_{e_1}[Y \mid X]$, which
encodes the benefit of the intervention on the alerted region, lowers risk
scores there, and on redeployment removes the alerts that produced the benefit.
This is the updating failure of \citet[Theorem~1]{liley2021model}, and an
instance of the suboptimality of naive retraining under
performativity~\citep{kabra2024limitations}; the misattribution supplies the
reason a monitoring team would retrain.

\section{A decomposition with the model as a policy}
\label{sec:estimand}

\todo{About two pages. Write the estimand in prediction-under-interventions
language~\citep{keogh2024prediction, luijken2024estimands}.}

\paragraph{Decision endpoint.} The quantity that matters for the update
decision is not $\Delta R$ but the change in policy value
$V_e(\pi) = \E_e[Y(\pi)]$ (or net benefit at threshold $\tau$,
$\mathrm{NB}_e(\tau)$~\citep{vickers2006decision}). A model can lose
discrimination while improving outcomes; that is the premise.

\paragraph{Decomposition.} Write the observed change as
\begin{equation}
\Delta R = \Delta_X^{\pi_0} + \Delta_{Y|X,A}^{\pi_0}
          + \Delta_{\pi}^{e_1},
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_\pi = R_{e_1}(f; \pi_1) - R_{e_1}(f; \pi_0)$ is a policy contrast
evaluated entirely within the post-deployment environment, so it never requires
$e_0$ outcomes under $e_1$ actions. The first two terms describe the change
under standard care. They are exogenous unless deployment itself moves the
patient mix, in which case the $X$ term absorbs that shift
(Section~\ref{sec:sim}, F5). They form a Shapley game over
$\{P(X), P(Y \mid X, A)\}$ with the policy fixed at $\pi_0$, identified when
the standard-care responses it needs have support in both environments and
post-deployment outcomes under $\pi_0$ are observed, as in a randomised
unalerted arm. Their sum and the policy term are differences of mean losses;
only the split of the sum needs a density ratio between the periods, and on
MIMIC-IV that split varies too much between samples to interpret, while the
sum and the policy term do not (Appendix~\ref{app:mimic}). The exact identity assigns interactions by a
policy-last ordering; no residual is omitted. This is an asymmetric Shapley
value~\citep{frye2020asymmetric} with the orderings restricted to those that
put the policy last. The restriction encodes the decision, not a causal order:
the policy's post-deployment value is the decision contrast in the current
patient mix.

\paragraph{Output.} A three-term decomposition with uncertainty intervals and a
sensitivity analysis, not a categorical label.

\section{Identification}
\label{sec:ident}

\todo{About two pages.}

\paragraph{Non-identification from routine data.} From observational
post-deployment data alone, $\Delta_\pi$ is not identified: improvement after
deployment is consistent with the model-triggered action, a concurrent quality
initiative, a change in treatment availability, or an unmeasured population
change. \todo{State as a proposition with a two-line non-identifiability
example.}

\paragraph{Logging requirements.} Risk score, alert exposure, acknowledgement,
action, timing, outcome.

\paragraph{Sources of identifying variation, strongest first.}
(1) Patient-level randomisation of the alert (an unalerted holdout arm):
identifies $\Delta_\pi$, the prospective contrast, by design.
(2) Site-level rollout: with random rollout order, a stepped wedge identifies
the retrospective contrast and the split of the covariate shift into
deployment-caused and exogenous parts at crossover; with non-random order it
does so only if trends in the loss, not merely in the covariates, are parallel
(Table~\ref{tab:identification}).
(3) Alerting-threshold discontinuity: identifies the \emph{local} effect at
$\rhat = \tau$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:rd}).
(4) Site or physician practice variation: may identify $\Delta_\pi$ under
instrumental-variable assumptions; not studied here.

\begin{proposition}[Local-to-global gap under threshold discontinuity]
\label{prop:rd}
Let the alert fire when $\rhat \geq \tau$, let $\pi_1 = \pi_0$ below $\tau$,
and let $\theta(r) = \E[Y(\pi_1) - Y(\pi_0) \mid \rhat = r]$ be the
intention-to-treat effect curve of the alert on the event probability, with
$F$ the distribution of $\rhat$ after deployment and
$\bar F(\tau) = P(\rhat \geq \tau)$. If $\E[Y(\pi_0) \mid \rhat = r]$ is
continuous at $\tau$, the post-deployment jump
$\lim_{r \downarrow \tau} \E[Y \mid \rhat = r] -
\lim_{r \uparrow \tau} \E[Y \mid \rhat = r]$ identifies $\theta(\tau)$. The
policy's effect on the event rate is
$\Theta = \int_{r \geq \tau} \theta(r)\, dF(r)$, and for the Brier score the
policy term of~\eqref{eq:decomp} is
$\Delta_\pi = \int_{r \geq \tau} (1 - 2r)\, \theta(r)\, dF(r)$; the
discontinuity alone identifies neither. (a) If $\theta$ is constant on
$[\tau, 1]$, then $\Theta = \theta(\tau)\, \bar F(\tau)$ and
$\Delta_\pi = \theta(\tau) \int_{r \geq \tau} (1 - 2r)\, dF(r)$. (b) If $\theta$
has constant sign and $|\theta|$ is monotone on $[\tau, 1]$, then
$|\theta(\tau)|\, \bar F(\tau)$ bounds $|\Theta|$ from below when the effect
grows with the score and from above when it shrinks.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Reading.} The first statement is standard regression-discontinuity
identification, which \citet{gallo2024effectiveness} use to evaluate a deployed
deterioration alert. The proposition adds the gap between that local effect and
the two global quantities, and the weight $1 - 2r$ that separates the
Brier-scale term from the effect on the event rate; closing the gap away from
the threshold needs assumptions beyond continuity, such as the conditional
independence of \citet{angrist2015wanna}. Which case applies is a clinical
question: the effect shrinks above the threshold when standard care already
treats most high-score patients. In our simulation the curve is neither constant nor monotone: the
effect strengthens from $-0.34$ just above the threshold to $-0.42$, then
fades to near zero at high scores, and the local jump overstates the average
effect among alerted patients by $14\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:rd}).

\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:sim}

\paragraph{Patient world model.} $X \in \mathbb{R}^2$ standard normal; the
deployed model $f$ is a fixed logistic score $\rhat$ along direction $w_r$;
clinical suspicion $s(X)$ is a linear index along a direction at angle $\phi$
to $w_r$, so the model flags patients the clinician misses. Standard care
$\pi_0$ treats suspicious patients ($s > \tau_s$) with probability $0.8$ and all
others with probability $\varepsilon$; the alert policy $\pi_1$ treats
$\rhat > \tau_r$ with probability $0.85$. The \emph{alert-only region}
($\rhat > \tau_r$, $s \le \tau_s$) holds $28\%$ of patients: it is where the
model has clinical value and where $\pi_1 / \pi_0 = 0.85 / \varepsilon$. Outcomes
are Bernoulli with logit $w_y^\top x + b_0 - \theta A$, $\theta = 2.5$; the
untreated event rate is about $40\%$. Exogenous shifts are a mean shift of $0.5$
in each coordinate of $X$ (S1) and a shift of $+0.6$ in the outcome logit
intercept (S2); they raise the Brier score by $0.051$ and $0.014$, against
$0.027$ for deployment alone (S3); S4 combines all three. Every estimator is
evaluated against coalition values computed from the known mechanisms as
expected Brier scores over $4 \times 10^6$ covariate draws per hybrid
environment (four independent blocks, whose spread gives the Monte Carlo
error), which an analyst cannot do. Operating point, seeds and code are
released.

\paragraph{Estimators.} (i) The union-graph importance-sampling estimator of
Zhang et al.\ with players $\{P(X), \pi, P(Y \mid X, A)\}$, unclipped and with
weights clipped at $100$; (ii) the two-player game $\{P(X), P(Y \mid X)\}$ that a
monitoring pipeline without action logs computes, with the covariate density
ratio estimated by a classifier; (iii) the proposed decomposition
(Section~\ref{sec:estimand}) with the policy term identified from a randomised
unalerted arm ($30\%$ of post-deployment patients). Each environment has
$n = 20{,}000$ patients. Every row of every sweep has its own seed block,
so no bias claim across rows reuses patients. Numbers are means over
independent replicates (100 per scenario for attribution, 30 per positivity
level, 20 per utility cell, 30 per triage level) with 95\% percentile bootstrap
intervals over replicates in brackets; these measure the Monte Carlo error of
the mean. What an analyst would report from one dataset is wider: each
replicate also gets a patient-level bootstrap, whose coverage of each
estimator's own estimand is in Appendix~\ref{app:intervals}.

\paragraph{F2: more data makes the wrong answer more confident
(Figure~\ref{fig:positivity}).} At $\varepsilon = 0$ the union-graph estimator
returns $-0.0088$ [$-0.0090$, $-0.0086$] against a target of $+0.0269$, with an
effective sample size of $15{,}993$ of $20{,}000$ and no extreme weight: the
pre-deployment sample contains no treated patient in the alert-only region, so
the estimator silently omits the cell, the usual consequence of a structural
positivity violation~\citep{petersen2012positivity}. Scaling $n$ from $2 \times 10^4$ to
$4 \times 10^5$ leaves the bias at $-133\%$ of the target while an analyst's 95\%
bootstrap interval narrows from [$-0.0102$, $-0.0077$] to
[$-0.0091$, $-0.0085$] (medians over replicates) and excludes the target in
every replicate; at $n = 4 \times 10^5$ the alert-only cell holds about
$112{,}076$ patients, none treated. Effective sample size is not monotone
in $\varepsilon$ and is highest where the estimate is wrong: it measures the
variance regime, not identifiability. Clipping bias is confined to the
intermediate regime $\varepsilon \in [10^{-4}, 10^{-3}]$, where weights are large
enough to matter and small enough to be sampled; at $\varepsilon = 0$ and
$\varepsilon \ge 10^{-2}$ clipping is a no-op. The proposed decomposition
recovers its estimand at every $\varepsilon$, with analyst-interval coverage
between $93\%$ and $100\%$.

\paragraph{F3: acting on the misattribution (Figure~\ref{fig:retrain}).}
A team that reads the monitor's attribution as outcome-mechanism drift refits
$f$ on post-deployment $(X, Y)$ and redeploys it. We compare seven update
rules over eight rounds against one standard-care reference, under the
deployed fixed threshold and with the original alert rate held, for a
homogeneous effect, a heterogeneous effect ($\kappa = 2$: the logit effect
varies along the covariate direction the score ignores) and a score class that
cannot represent the untreated risk (Appendix~\ref{app:retrain}). Under the
fixed threshold naive refitting fails in every setting. With a homogeneous
effect it enters the period-2 cycle predicted by Theorem~1 of
\citet{liley2021model}: events averted fall from $11.46$ [$11.43$, $11.49$]
percentage points to $0.01$ in round two and then alternate between about
$7.5$ and $0.1$, as the alert rate alternates between about $29\%$ and $9\%$.
The dashboard moves in antiphase with the benefit: observed AUROC in the
deployed population is $0.737$ to $0.744$ in the rounds in which the alert
averts almost nothing and $0.658$ to $0.660$ in the rounds in which it works,
against $0.644$ for the original score. Holding the alert rate
fixed removes the loss under a homogeneous effect ($11.33$ [$11.27$, $11.39$]
at round eight) but hides a calibration loss: the refitted score is on average
$0.159$ below the untreated risk. Under a heterogeneous effect the rate-held
naive refit keeps $5.42$ [$5.36$, $5.48$] of $8.07$ points, so holding the rate
is a governance lever, not a fix. The corrections form a hierarchy. Conditioning
on the action with main effects only fails under heterogeneity ($5.72$ points
with the fixed threshold, $5.47$ rate-held, no better than the rate-held naive
refit), because one treatment coefficient averages the effect over the deployed
population; adding
treatment-by-covariate interactions restores $8.08$. Refitting below the alert
threshold needs no action log and holds while the score class is well
specified; with a misspecified untreated risk and the fixed threshold it keeps
$5.77$ [$5.50$, $6.04$] of $9.44$ points. Refitting on untreated patients and
conditioning with interactions lose $1.71$ [$1.60$, $1.83$] points in that
setting: when the score class cannot represent the untreated risk, the fit
depends on which patients the current policy leaves untreated. Weighting the
untreated patients by the inverse of their probability of remaining untreated,
the marginal-structural-model weighting that \citet{sperrin2018msm} use for
treatment drop-in, removes that dependence and keeps the benefit in all six
settings ($9.50$ [$9.40$, $9.59$] misspecified, fixed threshold); it needs the
alert exposure and either the response probabilities or a model for them
(Appendix~\ref{app:retrain}). Refitting on untreated or unalerted patients is
an observational version of the holdout update of \citet{liley2021model} and
\citet{haidarwehbe2025holdout}, whose holdout is a random subset of patients
withheld from the score. Our addition is the comparison of these updates under
heterogeneous effects and a misspecified score class, which shows that the
weighting is what removes the dependence on the current policy.
Every action-log correction assumes the logged covariates capture the
clinician's treatment decision. How much naive refitting loses depends on how
many events the action prevents: at the $1\%$ event rate of the MIMIC-IV
cohort it averts a sixth fewer events than refitting on untreated patients and
does not oscillate, and weighting the untreated patients does not help (F7).

\paragraph{F4: attribution-guided refitting (Table~\ref{tab:decision},
Figure~\ref{fig:decision}).} Does the attribution improve the refit decision?
After the monitoring window of each scenario a rule keeps or naively refits
the score, and we count events in the next window, in which alerts are acted
upon and the exogenous state of the monitoring window persists; in S1 and S2
the score ran silently during monitoring. Each scenario has its own seed block
of 200 replicates, and the rules share draws within a replicate. Always
refitting loses $11.5$ points in S3 and in S4. The exogenous-share rule, refit when the
proposed decomposition assigns more than half of the change to exogenous
mechanisms, never refits in S3, keeps the gain of refitting in S2 ($16.8$

also refits in S1, where the original score is still the untreated risk:
naive refitting on standard-care data then imports standard care's treatment
of suspicious patients into the score, lowers the alert rate from $67\%$ to

\paragraph{F5: deployment also moves the patient mix
(Figure~\ref{fig:triage}).} Adding a triage effect, a covariate shift caused by
deployment itself, breaks Assumption~\ref{ass:switch} a second way: one event
moves both $\pi$ and $P(X)$, so the two cannot be switched separately. The
monitor books the whole
observed mix shift to $P(X)$, and so does the $X$ term of the proposed
decomposition, whose exogenous game sees only the realised covariate ratio.
Under a mixed shift the oracle Shapley target for $P(X)$ \emph{decreases} with
triage while the
monitor's attribution increases. Two policy estimands separate: the
retrospective contrast (undo deployment, including its triage effect) grows
from $+0.027$ to $+0.094$ as triage rises to $0.5$, while the prospective
contrast (switch the alert off in the current mix), which the keep-or-retrain
decision needs, grows only to $+0.043$; the randomised arm recovers the
prospective one at every triage level (largest gap $0.0009$, every 95\% interval of the gap includes zero). Patient-level randomisation cannot
separate exogenous from deployment-caused mix shift, because both arms draw
from the post-deployment referral stream. A site rollout supplies undeployed
comparisons if site identities and rollout dates are recorded. In a stepped
wedge with persistent sites (48 sites, six periods, 200 independent
seeds per setting; Table~\ref{tab:sites}), a crossover difference in
differences recovers the retrospective term when rollout order is random (bias
$-0.2\%$ [$-1.3$, $0.9$] with common covariate trends, $-0.7\%$ [$-1.7$, $0.4$]
with site-specific trends). Its Brier-scale split into a deployment-caused and
an exogenous covariate part is also unbiased under random order (within
$2.1\%$) if the exogenous part is taken from the not-yet-deployed sites' loss
change; imputing their average covariate shift into each site's loss instead
is biased by $+47\%$ once trends are site specific. When early rollout goes to
sites whose characteristic also sets case mix and baseline risk, the
retrospective term is biased by $-10.5\%$ [$-11.5$, $-9.5$] even with common
covariate trends, because the same covariate trend moves the Brier score by
different amounts at sites with different risk: parallel covariate trends do
not give parallel loss trends. With site-specific trends the covariate-mean
split is biased as well ($+16.5\%$ induced, $-36.5\%$ exogenous). Random
rollout order, not site-level rollout as such, identifies the split
(Section~\ref{sec:ident}; Appendix~\ref{app:sites}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Attributing performance change.} \citet{zhang2023attributing}
attribute a change in model performance to distribution shifts with a
cooperative game whose players are the mechanisms of a causal factorisation,
valued by Shapley values and estimated by importance weighting.
\citet{budhathoki2021rootcauses} attribute a change in a joint distribution to
changes in its causal mechanisms; \citet{cai2023disde} decompose a performance
drop into covariate-shift terms and a term for a changed relationship between
features and outcome; \citet{singh2024hierarchical} split a performance gap
into covariate and outcome-conditional shifts and attribute each to individual
variables without a causal graph; \citet{gordaliza2025causal} apply causal
attribution to performance gaps in medical imaging. All treat every mechanism
as exogenous to the audited model.

\paragraph{Performativity and model updating.} \citet{perdomo2020performative}
formalise performative prediction, in which a deployed model changes the
distribution it is evaluated on, and analyse repeated retraining.
\citet{kabra2024limitations} show that naive retraining can be provably
suboptimal under such shifts, and \citet{lee2026partially} let the distribution
respond both to the model and to exogenous drift. For clinical risk scores,
\citet{liley2021model} show that naive updating either converges to a score
that predicts its own effect or oscillates between two values, and propose a
holdout set, patients for whom the score is not computed, as a safe update
strategy; \citet{haidarwehbe2025holdout} derive the optimal size of that set.
\citet{boeken2024evaluating} treat the deployment of a decision support system
as a causal domain shift, define deployment and retraining effects, and give
conditions under which they are identified, assuming that deployment leaves the
covariate distribution unchanged. We use the holdout device for attribution and
their absolute-continuity condition to locate where the game loses
identification; the site-level design of Section~\ref{sec:ident} separates the
deployment-caused covariate shift that their assumptions exclude.

\paragraph{Prediction under interventions.} A line of work defines the estimand
for a model whose predictions trigger treatment~\citep{sperrin2018msm, vangeloven2020prediction, lin2021scoping,
keogh2024prediction, luijken2024estimands}, and several authors warn that a
successful model can become a victim of its own success~\citep{lenert2019victims,
sperrin2019explicit, balcarcel2025feedback}. Treatment use biases
validation~\citep{pajouheshnia2017accounting}, standard performance metrics do
not measure the reduction in outcomes a model brings~\citep{schuler2020performance},
and an accurate model can harm patients through self-fulfilling prophecies
while its discrimination stays intact~\citep{vanamsterdam2025selffulfilling}.
This work targets prediction and validation, not the attribution of a
monitored change.

\paragraph{Monitoring and governance.} \citet{feng2024designing} compare
monitoring strategies for a deployed algorithm under performativity, across
monitoring criteria and observational or interventional data. Effective
interventions confound post-market surveillance and can make a model look
decayed~\citep{ansari2025challenges}; feedback-aware monitors estimate
performance by injecting randomisation or by weighting~\citep{corbin2023avoiding,
kim2025monitoring}, or monitor conditional rather than marginal
performance~\citep{feng2024monitoring}. Regulatory and
post-market work analyses device updates~\citep{wu2024regulating}, the
regulation of devices that evolve~\citep{gonzalez2024regulating} and
governance infrastructure~\citep{afdideh2026aegis}, and
\citet{guan2026detecting} detect degradation under data shift.
These works detect, estimate or govern a change; they do not attribute it.

\paragraph{Evaluations of deployed alerts.} Alerts change care, and their effect on
outcomes varies. In a trial that randomised patients at six hospitals, alerts
for acute kidney injury changed some care processes but not the primary
outcome~\citep{wilson2021aki}; in a trial that randomised 74 clinical units in
two health systems, an early warning system built on nursing surveillance data
lowered in-hospital mortality and length of stay~\citep{rossetti2025concern}.
Observational evaluations use the other designs of Section~\ref{sec:ident}: a
staggered rollout compared patients reaching the alert threshold at hospitals
where a deterioration alert was live with those at hospitals where it was not
yet deployed~\citep{escobar2020automated}, and a regression discontinuity at
the threshold of a deployed deterioration score estimated the alert's local
effect~\citep{gallo2024effectiveness}. These studies estimate the alert's
effect on outcomes; Section~\ref{sec:ident} uses the same designs to identify
its share of a monitored performance change.

\section{Limitations and responsible-use statement}
\label{sec:responsible}

\todo{Required by the workshop; a missing statement is grounds for desk
rejection. Half a page.}

\paragraph{Limitations.} Results are theoretical and synthetic, or
semi-synthetic where MIMIC-IV covariates, outcomes and temporal drift are used
with a simulated deployment; no real deployment logs are analysed. In the
MIMIC-IV experiment the alert and its effect are simulated, the periods are
anchor year groups that date an admission only to within several years
(Appendix~\ref{app:mimic}), and the split of the real drift into covariate and
outcome terms has no ground truth and varies too much between samples to
interpret. The calibrated
operating point is deliberately strong: an acted-upon alert cuts the odds of
the event by a factor of about 12 ($\theta = 2.5$), $28\%$ of patients are
flagged only by the alert, and the untreated event rate is about $40\%$.
Magnitudes such as events averted scale with these choices: at the MIMIC-IV
event rate near $1\%$, naive refitting averts a sixth fewer events than
refitting on untreated patients instead of collapsing (F7). The direction of the misattribution does not change
(Proposition~\ref{prop:misattr}), and
Figure~\ref{fig:utility} and Table~\ref{tab:prevalence} vary the effect and the
event rate. The decomposition is order-dependent in its split of interaction
terms. The threshold discontinuity identifies only a local effect, and the
conditions under which it extends to the policy term fail in our own
simulation. A randomised unalerted arm assumes no interference between arms,
which shared ward staff, alert fatigue and clinicians' learning can violate.

\paragraph{Responsible use.} From routine observational post-deployment data,
model-mediated improvement is not identifiable; any attribution produced
without a source of identifying variation is a sensitivity analysis, not
evidence. Misattribution has a concrete clinical cost in both directions:
attributing performative success to outcome-mechanism shift retrains an
effective model and removes its benefit; attributing a genuine outcome shift to
the model's success leaves a degraded model in place, and on MIMIC-IV the
benefit of a simulated alert hid most of a real deterioration (F7). A
randomised unalerted arm withholds a possibly beneficial alert from some
patients; its size trades that cost against the precision of the attribution,
a trade-off \citet{haidarwehbe2025holdout} analyse for model updating. At
ward-deterioration event rates the trade-off is steep: on MIMIC-IV a $30\%$
arm among about $69{,}000$ admissions estimates the policy term with a
single-run SD half the term's size. The method requires
logging of alert exposure and action, which has privacy and workflow
implications that should be governed under existing post-market surveillance
frameworks~\citep{gonzalez2024regulating}. Nothing here should be read as
supporting the deployment or retention of a model without prospective
evaluation of patient outcomes.

\section{Persistent-site stepped wedge}
\label{app:sites}
The site-rollout experiment follows 48 sites for six periods. Four cohorts of 12 sites cross
at periods 2--5; the period-5 cohort supplies not-yet-deployed controls for the
three earlier crossovers. A fixed site characteristic $z$ shifts the site's
covariate mean by $0.5z$ and its outcome logit by $0.4z$. Rollout priority is
random, or $0.85z$ plus noise (correlation between early rollout and $z$ about
$0.78$); the covariate trend is common ($0.1$ per period) or site specific
($0.1 + 0.04z$ per period). Deployment adds a triage shift of $0.25$ to each
covariate. A difference in differences of observed Brier losses against
not-yet-deployed sites estimates the retrospective term at crossover, and the
controls' mean covariate change gives the exogenous increment of the
covariate-mean split. Two Brier-scale split estimators are compared. The
plug-in estimator reweights the switching site's pre-rollout losses under a
Gaussian location shift, to the controls' average covariate shift for the
exogenous part and to the site's own shift for the total. The DiD-anchored
estimator takes the exogenous part from the controls' observed loss change
and the deployment-caused part as the reweighted loss change at the site's
own shift minus that control change. Simulator counterfactuals are used only
to measure bias.

\input{tables/e5_sites}
With random rollout order every estimate is unbiased except the plug-in split
under site-specific trends ($+47\%$ exogenous, $-15\%$ induced), which imputes
the average trend into each site's loss although the loss responds to a trend
differently at sites with different $z$. With order linked to $z$, the
retrospective difference in differences is biased ($-10.5\%$ with common
trends, $-5.6\%$ with site-specific ones), because parallel covariate trends do
not produce parallel Brier trends when baseline risk differs, and the
DiD-anchored split inherits that bias; the plug-in split is unbiased with
common trends, where its location model holds at every site. No estimator is
unbiased in the linked, site-specific cell. These results concern the
crossover cohorts with a not-yet-deployed comparison; the last cohort's effect
is not identified without an additional period or assumption.

\section{Update rules after deployment (Experiment C)}
\label{app:retrain}
Each seed draws an evaluation population and an independent calibration
population of $20{,}000$ patients, and in each round a training sample of
$8{,}000$ patients treated under the current rule; all rules share these draws
(common random numbers), and each of the six settings has its own seed block
of 50 seeds. Events are expected values on the evaluation population, without
outcome noise, against standard care on the same population. The
heterogeneous effect is $\theta + \kappa (X_2 - X_1)/\sqrt{2}$ on the logit
scale; at $\kappa = 2$ the action is harmful for about $11\%$ of patients. The
misspecified truth adds the term of Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}, $0.65\{X_1X_2 + 0.3(X_2^2 - 1)\}$ to the
untreated logit; there the original score is the population-best logistic
approximation to the untreated risk, so a correct and stable update
reproduces it. The weighted refit uses $1/P(A = 0 \mid X)$ from the alert rule
and the response probabilities ($0.8$ for suspicious patients, $0.85$ for
alerted others, $\varepsilon$ otherwise). Estimating $P(A = 1 \mid X)$ instead,
by a logistic regression of $A$ on $X$, the alert and their interaction, changes
round-eight events averted by at most $0.01$ points in any setting.

\section{Semi-synthetic MIMIC-IV experiment}
\label{app:mimic}
\emph{Cohort.} Adult ward inpatients in MIMIC-IV~\citep{johnson2023mimic},
version 3.1~\citep{johnson2024mimiciv}, from
PhysioNet~\citep{goldberger2000physionet}.
One prediction time per admission, drawn at random among creatinine draws
taken on a ward (a care unit that is not an intensive care, emergency,
recovery, obstetric, newborn, psychiatric, discharge-lounge or unknown unit,
and in which MIMIC-IV records no ICU stay) at least four hours after
admission. Features: age, sex, emergency admission, hours since admission, a
prior ICU stay in the same admission, and the last value in the preceding
24 hours of creatinine, urea nitrogen, sodium, potassium, chloride,
bicarbonate, glucose, anion gap, white cells, haemoglobin and platelets, with
missing indicators. Outcome: ICU admission or in-hospital death within
12 hours. Pre-deployment period: patients with anchor year group 2008--2013;
post-deployment period: 2014--2019.
\emph{Simulated components.} The score is a logistic regression fitted to
$40\%$ of early-period patients; alerts fire at a $10\%$ alert rate on those
patients; an alert is acted upon with probability $0.85$; an acted-upon alert
prevents an event that would otherwise occur with probability $0.5$ and never
causes one; $30\%$ of post-deployment patients form a randomised unalerted
arm; the refit loop runs for eight rounds on post-deployment patients.
Everything else, including the outcome under standard care, is observed.
\emph{Targets.} The policy contrast and the total exogenous change are known
for the finite pools in expectation over the simulated components; the split
of the exogenous change into covariate and outcome parts has no ground truth
in real data and is reported, not scored.

\emph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:mimic} and Figure~\ref{fig:mimic} give the
main run and Table~\ref{tab:mimic-sens} the sensitivity runs; everything was
computed on the machine that holds the data, and only these aggregates left
it. The cohort has $180{,}910$ ward admissions of $89{,}340$ patients:
$114{,}952$ in 2008--2013, $53{,}647$ in 2014--2019 and $12{,}311$ in
2020--2022, used only in the run with that late period. ICU admission within
12 hours follows $1.17\%$ of prediction times and death $0.05\%$. The score is
fitted to $45{,}629$ early-period admissions; its AUROC is $0.738$ on the
held-out early admissions and $0.741$ in the late period, and its threshold, a
predicted risk of $0.023$, flags $9.6\%$ and $9.3\%$ of them.

\emph{Performance drop.} On the held-out early admissions the deployment
lowers the Brier score from $0.0106$ to $0.0091$, AUROC from $0.738$ to
$0.696$, observed over expected events from $0.98$ to $0.83$ and the
calibration slope from $1.01$ to $0.79$ (\texttt{performance\_drop.json}). In
the late period the score under-predicts under historical care (observed over
expected $1.23$; the event rate has risen), and the same deployment brings the
ratio to $1.05$: the alert's benefit hides the calibration drift as well as
the rise in the Brier score.

\emph{Precision.} The randomised arm recovers the policy term in every run,
with bias intervals that cover zero, but its single-run SD is $0.0007$ to
$0.0011$ in every scenario except drift and deployment with 2020--2022 as the
late period ($0.0021$ with $12{,}311$ admissions): as large as the term itself
when the action prevents a quarter of events.

\emph{Masking.} With real drift and deployment, the observed change in the
Brier score is $+0.00062$ in the main run and $+0.00135$ when the action
prevents a quarter of events, against a real deterioration of $+0.00229$; it
is $-0.00028$ when the action prevents three quarters and $-0.00030$ when the
alert flags $20\%$ of patients, so the deterioration then looks like an
improvement.

\emph{Union graph.} Every pre-deployment patient has $A = 0$, so the oracle
policy weight is $0.15$ for alerted and $1$ for other patients, and the
estimate measures how the alerted patients' losses enter the average, not what
the action does: it is identical, replicate by replicate, whether the action
prevents $25\%$, $50\%$ or $75\%$ of events, against policy contrasts from
$-0.00073$ to $-0.00220$.

\emph{Split of the real drift.} The total exogenous change is recovered in
every run, within $0.0004$ of its target. Its split into covariate and outcome
terms rests on a classifier's density ratio between the periods and is not
stable: in the main run each term has a single-run SD of $0.0037$ against a
total of $+0.0023$ (means $+0.0048$ for $P(X)$ and $-0.0025$ for the outcome
mechanism without deployment), and with periods by admission year or
2020--2022 as the late period the two terms reach $\pm 0.03$ to $\pm 0.08$,
with single-run SDs of the same size. The monitor and the proposed
decomposition share the ratio and the problem; the policy term and the total
need no ratio.

\emph{Update rules.} Under the fixed threshold, refitting on untreated
patients averts more events than naive refitting in every run, by $0.006$ to
$0.092$ points, the difference growing with the action's effect; whether naive
refitting beats keeping the score depends on whether recalibration to the late
period gains more than the prevented events cost ($-0.025$ to $+0.046$
points). With the alert rate held, no refit differs from keeping the score by
more than $0.03$ points. The weighted refit averts fewer events than the
unweighted refit on untreated patients in every run with the alert rate held
and in all but one with the fixed threshold, and its replicate SD is larger in
five of seven: with about 100 events in each training sample of $8{,}000$,
weights of $1/0.15$ on alerted untreated patients add variance.

\emph{Periods.} Anchor year groups date an admission only to within several
years. With periods by admission year, which drops admissions whose
three-year window straddles 2013 and 2014, the early and late periods hold
$66{,}683$ and $62{,}182$ admissions and every conclusion above holds. With
2020--2022 as the late period ($12{,}311$ admissions, event rate $1.5\%$) the
policy term and the exogenous change are recovered with wider intervals.

\section{Proofs}
For a binary outcome, write $\ell_y(x) = \ell(f(x), y)$; for the Brier score
$\ell_1 - \ell_0 = 1 - 2f(x)$.

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:absence}.} Let
$g_0(x) = P_{e_0}(Y{=}1 \mid X = x, A = a)$ on $\mathcal{X}_a$. Because
$\pi_0(a \mid x) = 0$ there, the distribution of $(X, A, Y)$ in $e_0$ does not
depend on $g_0$, and the distribution in $e_1$ depends only on the
post-deployment outcome mechanism; processes that differ only in $g_0$ give the
same data. A coalition $S$ with $m_A \in S$ and $m_Y \notin S$ puts mass
$p_S(x)\, \pi_1(a \mid x, f(x)) > 0$ on $(x, a)$, $x \in \mathcal{X}_a$, where
$p_S$ is the covariate density of the period that supplies $P(X)$, and its
expected loss there is $\ell_0(x) + g_0(x)\{\ell_1(x) - \ell_0(x)\}$. A
perturbation $\delta$ of $g_0$ therefore changes $v(S)$ by
$\int_{\mathcal{X}_a} p_S\, \pi_1\, (\ell_1 - \ell_0)\, \delta\, dx$, and the
weights $\pi_1/\pi_0$ are infinite on $\mathcal{X}_a$. Shapley values are linear
in $v$. With three players, $\{m_A\}$ and $\{m_A, P(X)\}$ enter $\phi_{m_A}$
with weights $1/3$ and $1/6$ and $\phi_{m_Y}$ with weights $-1/6$ and $-1/3$;
choosing $\delta$ with the sign of $\ell_1 - \ell_0$ moves both coalition values
the same way, so neither Shapley value is identified. The only non-identified
term of $\phi_{P(X)}$ is $\frac{1}{6}\{v(\{P(X), m_A\}) - v(\{m_A\})\}$, which
changes by
$\frac{1}{6} \int_{\mathcal{X}_a} (p_{e_1} - p_{e_0})\, \pi_1\,
(\ell_1 - \ell_0)\, \delta\, dx$; this vanishes for every $\delta$ only if
$p_{e_1} = p_{e_0}$ almost everywhere on the part of $\mathcal{X}_a$ where
$\ell_1 \neq \ell_0$. Assuming the outcome mechanism unchanged on
$\mathcal{X}_a$ sets $g_0$ equal to its post-deployment value, which $e_1$
identifies. $\square$

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:misattr}.} With $P(X)$ unchanged, switching only
$P(X)$ reproduces $e_0$ and switching only $P(Y \mid X)$ reproduces $e_1$, so
$v(\{P(X)\}) = 0$ and $v(\{P(Y \mid X)\}) = v(\mathcal{M}') = \Delta R$. Then
$\phi_{P(X)} = \frac12\{v(\{P(X)\}) - v(\emptyset)\} +
\frac12\{v(\mathcal{M}') - v(\{P(Y \mid X)\})\} = 0$, and by efficiency
$\phi_{P(Y \mid X)} = \Delta R$. The risk reduction on the alerted region makes
$P_{e_1}(Y \mid X) \neq P_{e_0}(Y \mid X)$ there, so the whole change is the
policy's. $\square$

\emph{Proposition~\ref{prop:rd}.} The policies agree below $\tau$, so
$\E[Y \mid \rhat = r] = \E[Y(\pi_0) \mid \rhat = r]$ for $r < \tau$ and
$\E[Y(\pi_0) \mid \rhat = r] + \theta(r)$ for $r \geq \tau$; with
$\E[Y(\pi_0) \mid \rhat = r]$ continuous at $\tau$, the jump is the right limit
of $\theta$ at $\tau$, written $\theta(\tau)$. $\Theta$ integrates the policy's
effect over $F$. For a prediction $r$ and binary $Y$,
$(r - Y)^2 = r^2 + (1 - 2r) Y$, so
$\Delta_\pi = \E[(1 - 2\rhat)\{Y(\pi_1) - Y(\pi_0)\}] =
\int_{r \geq \tau} (1 - 2r)\, \theta(r)\, dF(r)$. Nothing in the jump constrains
$\theta$ on $(\tau, 1]$, so neither quantity is identified without a
restriction. Case (a) is immediate. In case (b), $|\theta(r)| \geq
|\theta(\tau)|$ for $r \geq \tau$ when $|\theta|$ is nondecreasing, so
$|\Theta| \geq |\theta(\tau)|\, \bar F(\tau)$, and the inequality reverses when
$|\theta|$ is nonincreasing. $\square$
