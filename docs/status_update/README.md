## Post

Status on drift attribution: the experiment tasks E1 to E7 are done apart from the MIMIC-IV run, and every result now regenerates from a script (R1, R2, R4). The one thing we are missing is MIMIC-IV data: someone with credentialed PhysioNet access needs to run `sim2/run_e6.sh all <username>` on their own machine. ## Captions

**01. Where the project stands** (`01_workflow.png`)

Everything on the simulation side runs end to end and regenerates from saved result files, and the draft's numbers are checked against those files. The one missing input is MIMIC-IV (red): it needs a credentialed PhysioNet account and has to run on a local machine. The pipeline for it is written and tested (`sim2/run_e6.sh`), and an open stand-in (39,910 ICU stays from the PhysioNet 2019 challenge) is already done.

**02. Finding F1: the misattribution** (`02_attribution.png`)

When the only change is that clinicians act on the alert (left), the Brier score rises by +0.027. A monitor without action logs books all of it to the outcome mechanism; our decomposition, using a randomised unalerted arm, assigns it to the alert policy. With covariate and outcome shifts as well (right), the monitor's split is wrong in both terms, while ours matches what actually changed.

**03. Finding F2: more data does not help** (`03_more_data.png`)

When standard care never treated the patients the alert flags, the standard estimator settles at −0.009 while the true effect is +0.027. Going from 20,000 to 400,000 patients narrows its interval from 0.0025 to 0.0005 wide, and it covers the truth in 0% of runs at every size. Our decomposition recovers +0.027 from the randomised arm in the same setting.

**04. Finding F3: acting on the misattribution** (`04_retraining.png`)

Refitting the score on post-deployment data makes the benefit collapse every other round (11.5 → 0.0 → 7.7 → 0.1 events averted per 100 patients), and the dashboard AUROC looks best in exactly the rounds where the alert does nothing. Refitting on untreated patients, weighted by 1/P(untreated), keeps the benefit (11.4 at round 8).

**05. Finding F4: does attribution help the refit call?** (`05_refit_rules.png`)

Worst-case events lost against the better of keeping or refitting, over the four scenarios. Refitting only when our decomposition says the outcome mechanism changed loses 0.0; the same rule fed by the monitor loses 11.5, because it reads the alert's effect as outcome drift. Refitting when the exogenous share is over half (the rule in the to-do) loses up to 4.6, under pure covariate shift.

**06. Finding F6: discrimination and benefit move in opposite directions** (`06_auroc_vs_benefit.png`)

As clinicians act on more alerts, observed AUROC falls from 0.73 to 0.64 while events averted rise from 1.4 to 12.2 per 100 patients. A drop in AUROC after deployment can mean the alert is working.

**07. Prevalence decides the sign** (`07_prevalence.png`)

At a 40% event rate a successful alert raises the Brier score (+0.0187); at 14% and 7% it lowers it (−0.0076, −0.0065), while AUROC still falls. Either way the monitor books the change to the outcome mechanism, so at ward prevalence the alert's success reads as the outcome mechanism improving. Open team decision: how the intro frames the “performance drops” premise.

**08. Real covariates: open stand-in until MIMIC-IV** (`08_real_covariates.png`)

PhysioNet 2019 sepsis challenge data (two hospitals; real covariates and outcomes, the alert and its effect simulated). The alert lowers the Brier score by 0.0033 at hospital A and 0.0016 at B. The monitor books that to the outcome mechanism (−0.0032, −0.0014); our decomposition recovers it as the alert's effect (−0.0032, −0.0015). This stands in for the MIMIC-IV run; it does not replace it.
