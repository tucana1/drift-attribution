# Status update, 28 October 2026

Figures for the team channel, generated by `python3 sim2/make_status_update.py` from the result files in `figures/` (every number below is read from those files).

## Post

When clinicians act on a hospital AI alert, the model's measured accuracy changes even though nothing is broken, and we show that standard monitoring blames the wrong cause, while our method gets it right by holding the alert back for a random group of patients.

All the experiments are done, including the run on MIMIC-IV hospital records (180,910 ward admissions), which ran on our own computer and saved only summary numbers. On real patients with a simulated alert, standard monitoring again credits the alert's effect to lower underlying risk, and between 2008-13 and 2014-19 the alert's benefit hid 72% of a real rise in prediction error. What is left is writing the remaining sections of the draft.

When the patients the alert flags were never treated before it existed, the usual correction gets the alert's effect backwards (−0.009 instead of +0.027), and more data only makes it more confident.

`04_retraining.png`

Retraining on recent data teaches the model that treated patients are low risk, so the alert keeps switching off and on, and the accuracy score looks best exactly when the alert helps almost no one.

`05_refit_rules.png`

At our main simulation settings, deciding when to retrain with our method cost no extra bad outcomes, while the same rule using standard monitoring cost up to 11.5 extra per 100 patients; with a rarer bad outcome the two cost 0.5 and 0.9.

`06_auroc_vs_benefit.png`

The more often clinicians act on alerts, the more bad outcomes are prevented and the lower the model's measured accuracy, so falling accuracy after launch can mean the alert is working.

`07_prevalence.png`

When the bad outcome is uncommon (7 to 14% of patients, closer to real wards), a working alert lowers prediction error, which standard monitoring reads as patients' risk going down.

`08_real_covariates.png`

On real hospital records (MIMIC-IV ward admissions) and on public ICU data, with a simulated alert, standard monitoring mistakes the alert's effect for a change in risk, while our method gets it right.
